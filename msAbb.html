Manuscripts

The sigla Liebermann uses for the manuscripts and early printed texts of the laws (A for MS Harley 55, H for the Textus Roffensis…), with his German description, the manuscript's library and shelfmark, his dating, the laws it contains and his notes on it. Entries link here when they cite a manuscript.

About this list. This list is here to help readers of Liebermann’s Gesetze see which manuscripts he means, so that his edition, his apparatus and his notes can be followed. It is not a catalogue of the manuscripts, and it does not try to correct Liebermann point by point: a century of scholarship has changed much of what he says about dates, origins and the relations between manuscripts, and putting all of it right is beyond the scope of this edition. For a selection of the manuscripts, those that Patrick Wormald discusses in The Making of English Law (1999), his findings are set beside Liebermann’s, as the most direct way of showing how far scholarship has moved on since Liebermann wrote; other scholars are cited where their work bears directly on the edition (Bruce O’Brien on the Instituta Cnuti). Nothing here is meant to be complete, thorough or definitive, and not every detail can be guaranteed. Treat all of it, and Liebermann’s information above all, with caution: go back to Liebermann’s own text for what he actually says, and to the works cited and the libraries’ own catalogues before relying on any point. These tables are pointers for further research, not a source to be cited in their own right. In this the list follows Liebermann’s own method: the notes on language in his dictionary, he wrote, were offered “without any attempt at system, explanation, or completeness”, gathered first of all to help the editor find his own way (Foreword to vol. II). The same is true of this edition as a whole: it does not try to say everything, but to point the reader in the right direction.

The list follows Liebermann's Verzeichniss der einzelnen Handschriften and Fundort der Handschriften (Gesetze I, pp. xiii–xiv and xviii–xlii). Lower-case sigla (hb, lq…) are “posited by Liebermann”: manuscripts he infers from the readings that surviving manuscripts share. None survives, and later scholarship has rejected several of them (see Editorial Principles). “Lost” marks a manuscript known to have existed; “not seen by Liebermann” marks the few he knew only from others. Libraries are given by their present names (the British Museum is now the British Library, the Public Record Office The National Archives); private collections are as Liebermann knew them in 1903, except where a manuscript has since moved. For the manuscripts of the Old English laws and the Quadripartitus, the dates, notes and “Laws in it” also give Patrick Wormald’s findings (The Making of English Law I, 1999, ch. 4, pp. 162–263), which often correct Liebermann; each date and note says whose it is. Wormald’s dates use the palaeographers’ notation: s. xi is the eleventh century; s. xi1 and s. xi2 its first and second halves; s. xiin, s. ximed and s. xiex its beginning, middle and end; s. xii3/4 its third quarter; s. x/xi around 1000. A few transcripts and lost manuscripts that Wormald discusses but Liebermann did not know are added, marked “not in Liebermann”. For the Instituta Cnuti, the notes also give Bruce O’Brien’s findings (Anglo-Norman Studies 25, 2003), and two manuscripts Liebermann did not use (College of Arms Vincent 98, BL Harley 785) are added from him; see also the manuscripts of the Instituta.
