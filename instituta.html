Instituta Cnuti, read in order

The Instituta Cnuti aliorumque regum Anglorum, a Latin translation of Old English laws made soon after the Conquest (Liebermann: 1095–1135, probably 1103–20), read from beginning to end.

About this text and this page

Liebermann never prints the Instituta as one continuous text. Most of it translates the laws of Cnut, of Alfred and Ine, of Edgar, and a group of short texts on rank and oaths (Mirce, Að, Hadbot, Geþyncðo, Grið), and he prints each passage once only, as a Latin column beside the Old English it translates. His list of the work’s chapters (Gesetze I, pp. 612–615) gives in full only what has no Old English source, and points with a short quotation (“Si quis latro — — librum pęnitentialem; o. S. 343–47”) to the rest. This page puts the passages back together in the translator’s order, following that list. Each passage is labelled with its chapter of the Instituta and links to the clause page where Liebermann prints it, with his notes and the Old English beside it.

Numbering. The three books are Liebermann’s division. Books I and II translate I and II Cnut and are numbered to match them, so that In Cn II 54 translates II Cnut 54; the translator’s additions from other laws carry letters (In Cn I 12c is the third addition after Cnut’s I 12). Book III is a patchwork of Ine, Alfred, the texts on rank, and about a dozen chapters (III 45,4–55, 57–59) whose Old English source is lost. The rubrics are not the author’s and are given separately at the end.

Liebermann’s type. In the columns he prints beside the Old English, bold marks what differs from the source in content, spaced type what is reworded on purpose; smaller type marks words taken word for word from a source. Round brackets give a second version of the same words that Liebermann thought was also the author’s (more). These signs are being restored to the database text by text; some passages do not yet show them.

A caution. Liebermann’s edition of the Instituta has been criticised. Bruce O’Brien (‘The Instituta Cnuti and the Translation of English Law’, Anglo-Norman Studies 25 (2003), 177–97) found 54 errors of transcription in the title, prologue and first three chapters alone; showed that the manuscript Rl is only a copy of the Textus Roffensis (H), and that Cb is a reviser’s text which Liebermann trusted too far; and noted that Liebermann marks only the larger additions in bold, and never what the translator leaves out. He called for a new edition, which has not yet appeared. The text here is Liebermann’s, and should be read with that in mind.
