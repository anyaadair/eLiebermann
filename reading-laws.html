Reading the Laws

Each clause of the laws has its own page, with Liebermann's text, the other editions and translations, and Liebermann's notes. This guide explains the different kinds of note and how to follow Liebermann's references from one passage to another. It also explains the subject index, which lets you find every clause on a subject and see how that subject runs through the law codes.

Liebermann's notes · Following Liebermann's references · How to use the subject index

Liebermann's notes

Liebermann's notes are hidden until you open them: under the text on a clause page, click Show Liebermann's notes. The small raised numbers and letters in the text are his note marks. Click one to go to its note. Each note gives Liebermann's German as he printed it, then an English rendering made with an LLM (Claude) to help with the German, then where it is printed in the Gesetze.

His notes come from two volumes of the Gesetze, and are of three kinds.

Kind of noteWhere Liebermann printed itWhat it contains
Textual notesAt the foot of the page in vol. I (1903), under his textMostly the readings of the other manuscripts: what each has where it differs from the text printed. Their numbers start again on each page, and, where he prints several texts side by side, for each text.
Lettered notesAt the foot of the page in vol. I, in texts he prints without a German translation (for example Pseudo-Cnut De foresta)His explanations: sources, the manuscripts, the meaning of words, comparisons with other laws. Their letters, too, start again on each page.
Erklärungen (explanatory notes)In vol. III (1916), keyed to the raised numbers in his German translation in vol. IHis commentary on the content of the laws. Where a text has no German translation, they are keyed to marks in the text itself; in Pseudo-Cnut De foresta, for example, to the same letters as the notes in vol. I, so that one mark leads to both.

Following Liebermann's references

Liebermann often sends the reader from one note to another passage. On the clause page of Pseudo-Cnut De foresta, Prologue, for example, note c reads diese Zufügung soll Cnuts Landrecht zum Forstrecht stempeln, auch 11. 13s. 14. 17e. 18: “this addition is meant to stamp Cnut's general law as forest law; likewise 11, 13 note s, 14, 17 note e, 18”. Here the numbers are clauses of the same text, and a raised letter is a note at that clause: “13s” is clause 13, note s.

On this site these references are left as Liebermann printed them, in plain text, rather than turned into links. Linking them by machine would give wrong links in too many places, for three reasons:

  • They depend on context. “11. 13s” means clauses of the same text here. Elsewhere a bare number may mean a page (“o. S. 71”), a clause of a code named earlier in the note, or a line.
  • Letters and numbers restart on every page. So “13s” only makes sense once you know which clause 13 and which page's note s.
  • Liebermann mixes his systems. “S. 620g” is a page plus a note letter, “104” is a clause plus a note number, and “I 8c” is a part, a clause and a letter.

To follow a reference, first decide what kind it is:

  • A clause (a bare number, or a number after a law code's abbreviation): type the number into Go to a clause in the panel on the left of a clause or law code page. For another law code, open it from the list of law codes first.
  • A note at a clause (a raised number or letter after the clause number): go to the clause, open Liebermann's notes, and find the note with that number or letter.
  • A page (“S.” and a number): the page of the Gesetze where the passage is printed. Every note and every text on this site says which page it is from, and the law code pages give the pages of each code.

How to use the subject index

The subject index lists keywords assigned by hand to the clauses of the laws, for this database. Each clause has been read and given keywords of three kinds:

  • Legal matters: the offences, procedures and other matters the clause deals with (Theft, Homicide, Ordeal);
  • People: the kinds of people it concerns (Widow, Bishops, Thief);
  • Places: the places it names or concerns (Minster, London, Danelaw).

The index covers the pre-Conquest law codes, from Æthelberht to Cnut, but not the Anglo-Norman and Latin law-books. The keywords of a clause are also listed under Keywords at the foot of its page, and each leads to its subject.

Finding a subject

On the Subjects page the keywords are arranged in the three sections, and within each section in groups (for example Violence and injury under Legal matters). The number after a subject is the number of clauses indexed with it. Type in the box at the top to find a subject by name, and use Most clauses first or A–Z to change the order. A subject may appear in more than one section: Danelaw, for instance, is both a legal matter and a place.

A subject's page

Click a subject to open its page. It shows:

  • Through the law codes, a bar chart (described below);
  • Broader and Narrower subjects, where the subject belongs to a group with a broader keyword;
  • Often with: the keywords most often given to the same clauses, which suggest related subjects to explore;
  • The clauses: every clause indexed with the subject, law code by law code, each with its text and English translation. Click a clause number to open its page.

To search the words of these clauses, use Search within these clauses. It opens the search of the laws with the subject already chosen, so that you can add a word, or further keywords. With several keywords, All of them finds the clauses that have every one of them, and Any of them finds the clauses that have at least one.

Reading the bar chart

The chart Through the law codes shows how often a subject comes up in each law code, from the earliest to the latest.

  • Each bar is one law code, in order from left to right: the laws of each king in turn (named under the bars), then the undated and anonymous texts at the right, under Other texts.
  • The height of a bar is the number of clauses in that code indexed with the subject. The tallest bar is the code with the most such clauses; the number is given under the chart (“Tallest bar: 12 clauses”), and the other bars are drawn in proportion to it.
  • A flat, empty bar means that the code has no clause on the subject.
  • Point to a bar to see the code's name and the number of its clauses on the subject, out of all its clauses (for example “II Cn · II Cnut: 9 clauses of 160”). Click a bar to go to that code's clauses further down the page.

The bars count clauses, not words, and the codes are of very different lengths: Alfred's laws and II Cnut have far more clauses than the Kentish codes or the short anonymous texts. A tall bar may therefore reflect a long code rather than a particular interest in the subject. The figure “out of all its clauses”, shown when you point to a bar, helps to compare codes fairly. The chart shows only how the subject has been indexed, and it is a guide to where to look, not a measure of how important the subject was.
