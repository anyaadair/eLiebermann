Instituta Cnuti, read in order

The Instituta Cnuti aliorumque regum Anglorum, a Latin translation of Old English laws made soon after the Conquest (Liebermann: 1095–1135, probably 1103–20), read from beginning to end.

About this text and this page

Liebermann never prints the Instituta as one continuous text. Most of it translates the laws of Cnut, of Alfred and Ine, of Edgar, and a group of short texts on rank and oaths (Mirce, Að, Hadbot, Geþyncðo, Grið), and he prints each passage once only, as a Latin column beside the Old English it translates. His list of the work’s chapters (Gesetze I, pp. 612–616) gives in full only what he has not printed beside its source (chiefly what has no surviving Old English source, and a few chapters from Ine), and points with a short quotation (“Si quis latro — — librum pęnitentialem; o. S. 343–47”) to the rest. This page puts the passages back together in the translator’s order, following that list. Each passage is labelled with its chapter of the Instituta and links to the clause page where Liebermann prints it, with his notes and the Old English beside it.

Numbering. The three books are Liebermann’s division. Books I and II translate I and II Cnut and are numbered to match them, so that In Cn II 54 translates II Cnut 54; the translator’s additions from other laws carry letters (In Cn I 12c is the third addition after Cnut’s I 12). Book III is a patchwork of Ine, Alfred, the texts on rank, and about a dozen chapters (III 45,4–55, 57–59) whose Old English source is lost. The rubrics are not the author’s and are given separately at the end.

The manuscripts. The Instituta survives in two forms: a full text, running to III 64, and a short one that stops in the middle of III 44 (in the translation of Að). Liebermann (‘On the Instituta Cnuti’, 1893, §§ 24–27, and Gesetze I, p. 612) and Bruce O’Brien (2003) agree on the groups but not on every link between them. Each siglum below links to its entry in the list of manuscripts, which gives Liebermann’s information and O’Brien’s comments.

The full text
H, the Textus Roffensis (Liebermann: c. 1140; O’Brien: 1123–4), the earliest and, for Liebermann, the best text. Rl (c. 1200): Liebermann thought it a sister of H, copied from the same source, posited by him (hrl); O’Brien shows it is a copy of H, so its readings add nothing.
T (c. 1225) and Cb (Liebermann: c. 1160; O’Brien: c. 1175), both from a common source posited by Liebermann (tcb) and by O’Brien. Liebermann trusted Cb for old readings; O’Brien shows it is a reviser’s text, with parts of the Consiliatio Cnuti grafted in and many readings of its own.
The short text
Di, Bodleian Digby 13 (Liebermann: c. 1170–75; O’Brien: mid-12th century). A quire is lost, so Di has nothing for I Cn 13 to II Cn 15.
Ip Hunt: the copy someone inserted into Henry of Huntingdon’s Historia Anglorum (by c. 1190), at the reign of Cnut. It shares a common source with Di, posited by Liebermann (dhu) and by O’Brien. Its manuscripts are Lb (c. 1190–1200, the best; O’Brien’s ‘La’), S and Pl (from a common source posited by Liebermann, sp), Ct, Jo and Va. O’Brien adds two 16th-century transcripts Liebermann did not use, College of Arms Vincent 98 and BL Harley 785, which descend with Pl from a further lost manuscript.
Later copies
Copies of surviving manuscripts, with no independent value: Rc, Pa (16th-century copies close to Lb), Vl (from S), Ko (from Cb), Jl (extracts from H); Sin quotes two passages on Peter’s Pence.
How they are related
Liebermann put H, Rl and the short text together under one source he posited (hd), with T and Cb on their own. O’Brien’s family tree has three branches from the original: H (with Rl copied from it); T with Cb; and Di with the Huntingdon copies. He also argues that the short text is not a separate version by the author but a copy that broke off, so that every independent manuscript, of either form, is a witness to the one text.

In Liebermann’s notes, a run of sigla (“H Di Lb”) lists the manuscripts that share a reading. He prints them run together (“HDiLb”); here they are spaced so each can be read.

Liebermann’s marks. Liebermann uses the look of his print to tell the reader how the translation relates to the Old English beside it: bold type for what is changed in content, spaced type for what is reworded on purpose, smaller type for words taken over word for word, and round brackets for a second version of the same words that he thought was also the author’s. Here these are shown quietly, so that the text reads easily: plain black type is a faithful translation, and against it a pale wash marks what is changed, a fine underline what is reworded, grey type what is word for word from the source, and a dotted underline in brackets the other version. Hovering over a marked word gives a one-word reminder; the key above the text repeats them (more). Liebermann marks only the larger changes, and never what the translator leaves out.

The English crib. Beside the Latin is a relatively literal English crib, made with the help of an LLM (Claude, 2026) for this page. It is meant to help the reader through the Latin, not to replace it, and has not been checked line by line. Liebermann’s marks are carried over onto the English words that render the marked Latin, so that what the translator changed, reworded or took over can be followed in English too; where Latin and English word order differ, the match is approximate.

A caution. Liebermann’s edition of the Instituta has been criticised. Bruce O’Brien (‘The Instituta Cnuti and the Translation of English Law’, Anglo-Norman Studies 25 (2003), 177–97) found 54 errors of transcription in the title, prologue and first three chapters alone; showed that the manuscript Rl is only a copy of the Textus Roffensis (H), and that Cb is a reviser’s text which Liebermann trusted too far; and noted that Liebermann marks only the larger additions in bold, and never what the translator leaves out. He called for a new edition, which has not yet appeared. The text here is Liebermann’s, and should be read with that in mind.
