Manuscripts

The sigla Liebermann uses for the manuscripts and early printed texts of the laws (A for MS Harley 55, H for the Textus Roffensis…), with his German description, the manuscript's library and shelfmark, his dating, the laws it contains and his notes on it. Entries link here when they cite a manuscript.

The list follows Liebermann's Verzeichniss der einzelnen Handschriften and Fundort der Handschriften (Gesetze I, pp. xiii–xiv and xviii–xlii). Lower-case sigla (hb, lq…) are “posited by Liebermann”: manuscripts he infers from the readings that surviving manuscripts share. None survives, and later scholarship has rejected several of them (see Editorial Principles). “Lost” marks a manuscript known to have existed; “not seen by Liebermann” marks the few he knew only from others. Libraries are given by their present names (the British Museum is now the British Library, the Public Record Office The National Archives); private collections are as Liebermann knew them in 1903, except where a manuscript has since moved. For the manuscripts of the Old English laws and the Quadripartitus, the dates, notes and “Laws in it” also give Patrick Wormald’s findings (The Making of English Law I, 1999, ch. 4, pp. 162–263), which often correct Liebermann; each date and note says whose it is. Wormald’s dates use the palaeographers’ notation: s. xi is the eleventh century; s. xi¹ and s. xi² its first and second halves; s. xiⁱⁿ, s. xiᵐᵉᵈ and s. xiᵉˣ its beginning, middle and end; s. xii³ᐟ⁴ its third quarter; s. x/xi around 1000. A few transcripts and lost manuscripts that Wormald discusses but Liebermann did not know are added, marked “not in Liebermann”. For the Instituta Cnuti, the notes also give Bruce O’Brien’s findings (Anglo-Norman Studies 25, 2003), and two manuscripts Liebermann did not use (College of Arms Vincent 98, BL Harley 785) are added from him; see also the manuscripts of the Instituta.
